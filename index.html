Draw the widest street between two classes. Only the points on the curb matter.

A support vector machine predicts with \(f(\mathbf x)=\sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_iK(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_i)\) (Eq. 17.63), a weighted sum of kernels centered at the training points. The training problem is set up so that most \(\alpha_i\) come out exactly zero. The few points with \(\alpha_i\gt0\) are the support vectors, and at test time they are the only training points you need.

The route there: pick the separating line with the largest margin (17.3.1), rewrite that problem in terms of one multiplier per point (17.3.2), let some points break the rules for a price \(C\) (17.3.3), and swap every dot product for a kernel (17.3.4). Labels are \(\tilde y\in\{-1,+1\}\) throughout.

§17.3.1

Large margin classifiers: the widest street wins

A linear classifier predicts \(h(\mathbf x)=\operatorname{sign}(f(\mathbf x))\) with \(f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+w_0\) (Eq. 17.64). When the classes can be separated, infinitely many lines do it. The SVM picks the one with the largest margin: the distance from the boundary to the closest training point. A wide margin leaves room for the data to wobble without changing any prediction.

The signed distance of \(\mathbf x\) from the boundary is \(r=f(\mathbf x)/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\) (Eqs. 17.65–17.66). Rescaling \(\mathbf w,w_0\) doesn't move the line, so we fix the scale by asking the closest point to have \(\tilde y f=1\). Maximizing the margin \(1/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\) then becomes \(\min\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2\) subject to \(\tilde y_n(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n+w_0)\ge1\) for every \(n\) (Eq. 17.68).

Explain itAt your level

Build the widest road you can between two groups of houses.

Red houses are on one side of town, blue houses on the other. You have to build a straight road between them. Lots of roads would work, but a skinny road squeezed right next to a red house is risky: if a new red house gets built a little closer, it ends up on the wrong side.

So build the widest straight road that doesn't knock down any house. The houses touching the edges of the road are the important ones. They're the support vectors. You could erase every other house and the road would stay in exactly the same place.

One more thing: measure in the same units in every direction. If one direction is measured in inches and the other in miles, the "widest road" comes out silly.

Any straight line can be written as \(w_1x_1+w_2x_2+w_0=0\). Plug a point into the left side and you get a score \(f(\mathbf x)\). Positive means one side of the line, negative means the other. The actual distance from the line is the score divided by the length of the arrow \(\mathbf w=(w_1,w_2)\): \(r=f(\mathbf x)/\sqrt{w_1^2+w_2^2}\).

The margin of a line is the distance to its closest point. The SVM chooses the line where that closest distance is as big as possible. In the Play box, turn the line yourself and watch the margin, then press "Snap" to see the best one.

Doubling every \(w\) doubles every score but doesn't move the line, so the distances stay the same. The SVM uses that freedom to make the closest points score exactly \(\pm1\). After that, a big margin means a short arrow \(\mathbf w\).

Write any \(\mathbf x\) as its projection onto the boundary plus a step along the unit normal: \(\mathbf x=\mathbf x_\perp+r\,\mathbf w/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\). Since \(f(\mathbf x_\perp)=0\), \(f(\mathbf x)=r\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\), so \(r=f(\mathbf x)/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\).

Correct classification means \(\tilde y_nf(\mathbf x_n)\gt0\), and the margin is \(\min_n\tilde y_nf(\mathbf x_n)/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\). That's Eq. 17.67, a max-min problem. It is invariant to \((\mathbf w,w_0)\to(k\mathbf w,kw_0)\), so impose \(\min_n\tilde y_nf_n=1\). The objective becomes \(1/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\), and maximizing it is the same as minimizing \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2\): a convex quadratic program with \(N\) linear constraints (Eq. 17.68).

The width of the "street" between \(f=-1\) and \(f=+1\) is \(2/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\). Because distance uses every coordinate equally, standardize features first (Fig. 17.14).

  • Uniqueness. \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2\) is strictly convex in \(\mathbf w\) and the feasible set is a polyhedron, so \(\hat{\mathbf w}\) is unique whenever the data are separable. \(\hat w_0\) is unique too once there's at least one support vector from each class.
  • Geometry. The max-margin hyperplane is the perpendicular bisector of the shortest segment between the two classes' convex hulls. The hard-margin dual is equivalent to that nearest-points problem.
  • Why margin. Classical bounds for linear classifiers with \(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\le R\) and margin \(\gamma\) scale with \(R^2/\gamma^2\) instead of the dimension. This is why SVMs did well in very high-dimensional feature spaces.
  • Implicit bias. Gradient descent on the logistic loss for separable data converges in direction to the hard-margin SVM solution (Soudry et al., 2018). Max margin turns out to be what unregularized logistic regression is heading toward.
  • Scaling. The margin is a Euclidean quantity, so the solution is not invariant to per-feature rescaling. StandardScaler before SVC is not optional (Fig. 17.14).

Math, slowlyEquations 17.63 – 17.68

The model

\[ f(\mathbf x)=\sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_iK(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_i)\quad\text{(17.63)}\qquad h(\mathbf x)=\operatorname{sign}(f(\mathbf x)),\quad f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+w_0 \tag{17.64} \]

Section 17.3.4 shows these are the same thing: the linear \(f\) is 17.63 with a linear kernel, plus a bias.

Distance to the boundary

  1. Split \(\mathbf x\) into a point on the boundary plus a step along the unit normal: \[ \mathbf x=\mathbf x_\perp+r\frac{\mathbf w}{\lVert\mathbf w\rVert} \tag{17.65} \] \(\mathbf w\) is perpendicular to the boundary, so the shortest path off it runs along \(\mathbf w\). \(r\) is signed: positive on the side \(\mathbf w\) points to.
  2. Apply \(f\): \[ f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+w_0=(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_\perp+w_0)+r\frac{\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w}{\lVert\mathbf w\rVert}=(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_\perp+w_0)+r\lVert\mathbf w\rVert \tag{17.66} \] \(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w=\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2\), and one factor cancels.
  3. \(\mathbf x_\perp\) is on the boundary, so \(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_\perp+w_0=0\), leaving \[ r=\frac{f(\mathbf x)}{\lVert\mathbf w\rVert}. \] The boundary sits at signed distance \(-w_0/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\) from the origin. That's the "offset" slider in the Play box.

From max-min to a quadratic program

  1. We want every point on its correct side, \(\tilde y_nf(\mathbf x_n)\gt0\), and the closest one as far as possible: \[ \max_{\mathbf w,w_0}\ \frac{1}{\lVert\mathbf w\rVert}\min_{n=1}^N\big[\tilde y_n(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n+w_0)\big] \tag{17.67} \] Multiplying by \(\tilde y_n\) turns "on the correct side" into "positive" for both classes.
  2. Replace \((\mathbf w,w_0)\) by \((k\mathbf w,kw_0)\): the numerator and \(\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\) both scale by \(k\), so nothing changes. Choose \(k\) so the closest point has \(\tilde y_nf_n=1\). Try the "scale ‖w‖" slider and then the "Rescale" button: the line and the margin stay put while every \(f\) value changes.
  3. With that scale, every point has \(\tilde y_nf_n\ge1\), and the objective is \(1/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\). Maximizing it is minimizing \(\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\), which is minimizing \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2\): \[ \min_{\mathbf w,w_0}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\tilde y_n(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n+w_0)\ge1,\quad n=1:N \tag{17.68} \] Squaring removes the square root and makes the objective a smooth quadratic. The \(\tfrac12\) only tidies the derivative.

A worked example

Two points: \(\mathbf x_1=(1,0)\) with \(\tilde y=+1\) and \(\mathbf x_2=(-1,0)\) with \(\tilde y=-1\). By symmetry the boundary is the vertical axis, \(\mathbf w=(w,0)\), \(w_0=0\). The constraints say \(w\cdot1\ge1\) and \(-(w\cdot(-1))\ge1\), so \(w\ge1\). The smallest \(\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\) is \(w=1\), the margin is \(1/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert=1\), and the street from \(x=-1\) to \(x=1\) has width \(2/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert=2\). Both points are support vectors.

Why scale (Fig. 17.14): distance \(\sqrt{\Delta x_0^2+\Delta x_1^2}\) is dominated by whichever feature has the larger numbers. Stretch \(x_1\) by 20 and the max-margin problem mostly cares about \(x_1\), so the boundary goes nearly horizontal. Standardizing puts both features on equal footing.

§17.3.2

The dual problem: one multiplier per point, and most are zero

Problem 17.68 is the primal: \(D+1\) unknowns \((\mathbf w,w_0)\) and \(N\) constraints. Give each constraint a Lagrange multiplier \(\alpha_n\ge0\), eliminate \(\mathbf w\) and \(w_0\), and you get the dual: maximize \(\sum_n\alpha_n-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\tilde y_i\tilde y_j\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j\) subject to \(\alpha_n\ge0\) and \(\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n=0\) (Eq. 17.77).

The answer comes back as \(\hat{\mathbf w}=\sum_n\hat\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n\) (Eq. 17.73). The KKT condition \(\alpha_n(\tilde y_nf(\mathbf x_n)-1)=0\) says each point either has \(\alpha_n=0\) or sits exactly on the margin. The points with \(\alpha_n\gt0\) are the support vectors.

Explain itAt your level

Every house gets a "how hard am I pushing on the road" number.

Imagine each house can push on the edge of the road to keep it away. Houses far from the road don't touch it, so they push with zero force. Only the houses right at the edge push.

When everything settles, the road's direction is just the pushes added up. Red houses push one way, blue houses push the other way, and the pushes from the two sides balance out exactly.

The "dual problem" is a way of finding the road by figuring out how hard each house pushes. Most of the numbers come out zero, which is what makes it handy.

Instead of solving for the line directly, the SVM solves for one number \(\alpha_n\ge0\) per training point. Then the line's arrow is a weighted sum of the points: \(\mathbf w=\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n\). Red points (\(\tilde y=+1\)) add their position, blue points subtract theirs.

Two rules come out of the math. The weights on the red side and the blue side have to balance: \(\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n=0\). And a point only gets a nonzero weight if it is sitting right on the edge of the street, with score exactly \(\pm1\).

The Play box solves for the \(\alpha\)s one pair at a time. Step through it and watch the bars: most go to zero, and only the points on the dashed margin lines keep a bar.

Lagrangian: \(L=\tfrac12\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w-\sum_n\alpha_n(\tilde y_n(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n+w_0)-1)\). Setting \(\nabla_{\mathbf w}L=0\) gives \(\mathbf w=\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n\), and \(\partial L/\partial w_0=0\) gives \(\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n=0\). Substituting back removes \(\mathbf w\) and \(w_0\) and leaves a concave quadratic in \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) (17.77).

The data enter only through the inner products \(\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j\). That is the opening for the kernel trick in 17.3.4.

KKT (17.78–17.80): \(\alpha_n\ge0\), \(\tilde y_nf_n\ge1\), and complementary slackness \(\alpha_n(\tilde y_nf_n-1)=0\). Prediction needs only the support vectors (17.81). For \(\hat w_0\), any support vector satisfies \(\tilde y_nf_n=1\), so \(\hat w_0=\tilde y_n-\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\mathbf x_n\). Averaging over all of them (17.82) is more stable numerically.

  • Strong duality. The primal is convex with affine constraints, so Slater's condition (in its refined form for affine constraints) holds whenever the problem is feasible. Primal and dual optima are equal: \(\tfrac12\lVert\hat{\mathbf w}\rVert^2=\sum_n\hat\alpha_n-\tfrac12\lVert\hat{\mathbf w}\rVert^2\), so \(\sum_n\hat\alpha_n=\lVert\hat{\mathbf w}\rVert^2\). Check it in the Play box.
  • When the dual pays off. The dual has \(N\) variables, the primal \(D+1\). For a linear SVM with \(N\gg D\), solve the primal (LIBLINEAR, Pegasos SGD). The dual wins when \(D\) is huge or infinite, which is exactly the kernel case.
  • SMO. Because of \(\sum\alpha_n\tilde y_n=0\), the smallest update changes two multipliers, and that 2-variable QP has a closed-form solution. LIBSVM picks the pair with a second-order rule (Fan, Chen & Lin, 2005). That's the solver running in the Play box.
  • Sparsity ≠ uniqueness. \(\hat{\mathbf w}\) is unique but \(\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}\) need not be: with degenerate data (e.g. three collinear points on one margin) several \(\boldsymbol\alpha\)'s give the same \(\mathbf w\). The set of "support vectors" can depend on the solver.
  • Leave-one-out. Removing a non-support vector leaves the solution unchanged, so LOO error \(\le\#\text{SV}/N\).

Math, slowlyEquations 17.69 – 17.82

Lagrangian and stationarity

\[ L(\mathbf w,w_0,\boldsymbol\alpha)=\tfrac12\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w-\sum_{n=1}^N\alpha_n\big(\tilde y_n(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n+w_0)-1\big) \tag{17.69} \]
\[ (\hat{\mathbf w},\hat w_0,\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha})=\min_{\mathbf w,w_0}\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha\ge0}L(\mathbf w,w_0,\boldsymbol\alpha) \tag{17.70} \]
  1. Why the inner max enforces the constraints: if some \(\tilde y_nf_n-1\lt0\), sending \(\alpha_n\to\infty\) sends \(L\to\infty\). If all constraints hold, the best choice is \(\alpha_n=0\) wherever \(\tilde y_nf_n\gt1\), and \(L=\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2\). So \(\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha}L\) equals the primal objective on feasible points and \(+\infty\) elsewhere.
  2. Differentiate: \[ \nabla_{\mathbf w}L=\mathbf w-\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n\quad\text{(17.71)}\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_0}=-\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n \tag{17.72} \]
  3. Set both to zero: \[ \hat{\mathbf w}=\sum_n\hat\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n\quad\text{(17.73)}\qquad 0=\sum_n\hat\alpha_n\tilde y_n \tag{17.74} \] \(\hat{\mathbf w}\) lives in the span of the training points. The red and blue multipliers balance.

Substituting back

  1. Expand 17.69: \[ L=\tfrac12\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\hat{\mathbf w}-\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\mathbf x_n-w_0\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n+\sum_n\alpha_n \tag{17.75} \]
  2. The second sum is \(\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n=\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\hat{\mathbf w}\), and the third is \(w_0\cdot0\): \[ L=\tfrac12\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\hat{\mathbf w}-\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\hat{\mathbf w}-0+\sum_n\alpha_n \tag{17.76} \]
  3. Write \(\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\hat{\mathbf w}=\sum_i\sum_j\alpha_i\alpha_j\tilde y_i\tilde y_j\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j\): \[ L(\boldsymbol\alpha)=-\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N\alpha_i\alpha_j\tilde y_i\tilde y_j\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j+\sum_{n=1}^N\alpha_n \tag{17.77} \] Maximize over \(\alpha_n\ge0\) with \(\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n=0\). It's concave (the quadratic term is \(-\tfrac12\lVert\sum\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n\rVert^2\)). Generic QP solvers take \(O(N^3)\). SMO takes roughly \(O(N)\) to \(O(N^2)\).

KKT conditions and support vectors

\[ \alpha_n\ge0\quad\text{(17.78)}\qquad \tilde y_nf(\mathbf x_n)-1\ge0\quad\text{(17.79)}\qquad \alpha_n\big(\tilde y_nf(\mathbf x_n)-1\big)=0 \tag{17.80} \]

For each point, one of the two factors in 17.80 is zero. Either \(\alpha_n=0\) and the point doesn't contribute to \(\hat{\mathbf w}\), or \(\tilde y_nf_n=1\) and it sits on a margin line. Call the second set \(\mathcal S\).

\[ f(\mathbf x;\hat{\mathbf w},\hat w_0)=\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\mathbf x+\hat w_0=\sum_{n\in\mathcal S}\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n^\top\mathbf x+\hat w_0 \tag{17.81} \]
\[ \hat w_0=\frac1{|\mathcal S|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal S}(\tilde y_n-\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\mathbf x_n)=\frac1{|\mathcal S|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal S}\Big(\tilde y_n-\sum_{m\in\mathcal S}\alpha_m\tilde y_m\mathbf x_m^\top\mathbf x_n\Big) \tag{17.82} \]

Each term comes from \(\tilde y_n(\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\mathbf x_n+\hat w_0)=1\): multiply both sides by \(\tilde y_n\) and use \(\tilde y_n^2=1\).

The two-point example, from the dual side

\(\mathbf x_1=(1,0),\tilde y_1=+1\); \(\mathbf x_2=(-1,0),\tilde y_2=-1\). The constraint \(\alpha_1-\alpha_2=0\) forces \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha\). Every term \(\tilde y_i\tilde y_j\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j\) equals \(+1\), so

\[ L(\alpha)=2\alpha-\tfrac12\cdot4\alpha^2=2\alpha-2\alpha^2\ \Rightarrow\ \hat\alpha=\tfrac12,\quad \hat{\mathbf w}=\tfrac12(1,0)-\tfrac12(-1,0)=(1,0),\quad \hat w_0=1-1=0. \]

Same answer as the primal. The dual optimum \(L=1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12\) equals the primal optimum \(\tfrac12\lVert\hat{\mathbf w}\rVert^2=\tfrac12\), and \(\sum\hat\alpha_n=1=\lVert\hat{\mathbf w}\rVert^2\).

§17.3.3

Soft margin classifiers: pay C for every rule you break

If the classes overlap, no line satisfies \(\tilde y_nf_n\ge1\) for all \(n\) and the hard-margin problem has no solution. Give each point a slack \(\xi_n\ge0\) and require only \(\tilde y_nf_n\ge1-\xi_n\). Each unit of slack costs \(C\): minimize \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2+C\sum_n\xi_n\) (Eq. 17.83).

The dual is the same as before, except the multipliers are now boxed in: \(0\le\alpha_n\le C\) (Eq. 17.86). Points with \(0\lt\alpha_n\lt C\) sit on the margin. Points with \(\alpha_n=C\) are inside it or on the wrong side. Small \(C\) gives a wide, forgiving margin. Large \(C\) approaches the hard margin.

Explain itAt your level

Sometimes a few houses are in the way. Pay a fine and build the road anyway.

What if a red house was built in the middle of the blue neighborhood? Now no straight road can keep every red house on one side. So we make a deal: you're allowed to build the road over some houses' yards, or even put a house on the wrong side, but you pay a fine for each one. The farther over the line, the bigger the fine.

\(C\) is how big the fine is. A tiny fine means "build a nice wide road, I don't mind a few houses in the way." A huge fine means "don't you dare," and you get a skinny road that tries hard to keep everyone on their side.

Each point gets a "how much did I break the rule" number \(\xi_n\) (the Greek letter xi). If the point is outside the street on its own side, \(\xi_n=0\). If it's inside the street but still on the right side of the middle line, \(0\lt\xi_n\lt1\). If it's past the middle line, on the wrong side, \(\xi_n\gt1\).

The SVM now balances two goals: a wide street (small \(\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\)) and a small total of rule-breaking \(\sum\xi_n\). \(C\) sets the exchange rate. Every misclassified point has \(\xi_n\gt1\), so \(\sum\xi_n\) is at least the number of mistakes.

In the Play box, slide \(C\) and watch the street. The orange segments show each point's slack.

Primal (17.83): \(\min\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2+C\sum_n\xi_n\) s.t. \(\xi_n\ge0\), \(\tilde y_n(\mathbf x_n^\top\mathbf w+w_0)\ge1-\xi_n\). Lagrangian (17.84) adds multipliers \(\alpha_n\) for the margin constraints and \(\mu_n\) for \(\xi_n\ge0\). Setting \(\partial L/\partial\xi_n=C-\alpha_n-\mu_n=0\) and using \(\mu_n\ge0\) gives \(\alpha_n\le C\). The \(\xi\) terms cancel, and the dual objective is identical to 17.77.

Complementary slackness now reads \(\alpha_n(\tilde y_nf_n-1+\xi_n)=0\) and \(\mu_n\xi_n=(C-\alpha_n)\xi_n=0\). So \(\alpha_n=0\Rightarrow\tilde y_nf_n\ge1\); \(0\lt\alpha_n\lt C\Rightarrow\xi_n=0,\ \tilde y_nf_n=1\); \(\alpha_n=C\Rightarrow\xi_n=1-\tilde y_nf_n\ge0\).

Compute \(\hat w_0\) from the on-margin set \(\mathcal M=\{0\lt\alpha_n\lt C\}\) only (17.88), because only those points are known to satisfy \(\tilde y_nf_n=1\).

  • Hinge loss. At the optimum \(\xi_n=\max(0,1-\tilde y_nf_n)\), so 17.83 is \(\sum_n\ell_{\text{hinge}}(\tilde y_nf_n)+\tfrac1{2C}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2\): regularized empirical risk minimization with a convex surrogate of 0-1 loss. \(\lambda=1/C\) (up to an \(N\)).
  • Regularization path. \(\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}(C)\) is piecewise linear in \(C\) (Hastie et al., 2004), so the whole path costs about as much as one fit. The small charts in the Play box trace margin width and #SV along it.
  • ν-SVM. Replaces \(C\) with \(\nu\in(0,1]\) (17.89–17.92): \(\nu\) upper-bounds the fraction of margin errors and lower-bounds the fraction of support vectors. Every C-SVM solution is a ν-SVM solution for some \(\nu\) in the bracket the Play box shows.
  • Tuning. Search \(C\) on a log grid (and the kernel's scale jointly). With an RBF kernel, \((C,\gamma)\) trade off. Large \(C\) with small \(\gamma\) and small \(C\) with large \(\gamma\) can both fit well.
  • Class imbalance. Use per-class \(C_+,C_-\) (sklearn class_weight). The box constraint becomes \(0\le\alpha_n\le C_{\tilde y_n}\).

Math, slowlyEquations 17.83 – 17.92

The soft-margin primal

\[ \min_{\mathbf w,w_0,\boldsymbol\xi}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2+C\sum_{n=1}^N\xi_n\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\xi_n\ge0,\quad\tilde y_n(\mathbf x_n^\top\mathbf w+w_0)\ge1-\xi_n \tag{17.83} \]

\(C=\infty\) makes any slack infinitely expensive and recovers 17.68.

Lagrangian and the box constraint

\[ L=\tfrac12\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w+C\sum_n\xi_n-\sum_n\alpha_n\big(\tilde y_n(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n+w_0)-1+\xi_n\big)-\sum_n\mu_n\xi_n \tag{17.84} \]
  1. \(\nabla_{\mathbf w}L=0\) and \(\partial L/\partial w_0=0\) give the same results as before: \(\mathbf w=\sum\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n\), \(\sum\alpha_n\tilde y_n=0\).
  2. The new one: \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\xi_n}=C-\alpha_n-\mu_n=0\), so \(\alpha_n=C-\mu_n\). Since \(\mu_n\ge0\), \(\alpha_n\le C\). That is the only change.
  3. Plug in. The \(\xi_n\) terms are \(\xi_n(C-\alpha_n-\mu_n)=0\), so they vanish: \[ L(\boldsymbol\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_i-\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N\alpha_i\alpha_j\tilde y_i\tilde y_j\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j \tag{17.85} \] \[ 0\le\alpha_n\le C\quad\text{(17.86)}\qquad \sum_{n=1}^N\alpha_n\tilde y_n=0 \tag{17.87} \]

\[ \hat w_0=\frac1{|\mathcal M|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal M}\Big(\tilde y_n-\sum_{m\in\mathcal S}\alpha_m\tilde y_m\mathbf x_m^\top\mathbf x_n\Big),\qquad\mathcal M=\{n:0\lt\alpha_n\lt C\} \tag{17.88} \]

The ν-SVM

\[ \max_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\ -\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\tilde y_i\tilde y_j\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j\quad\text{s.t.}\quad 0\le\alpha_n\le\tfrac1N,\quad\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n=0,\quad\sum_n\alpha_n\ge\nu \tag{17.89–17.92} \]

Each \(\alpha_n\le1/N\), so to reach \(\sum\alpha_n\ge\nu\) at least \(\nu N\) of them must be nonzero: \(\nu\) is a lower bound on the fraction of support vectors. Margin errors (\(\xi_n\gt0\)) all sit at \(\alpha_n=1/N\), and at the optimum \(\sum\alpha_n=\nu\), so there are at most \(\nu N\) of them. Unlike \(C\), \(\nu\) is a number between 0 and 1 you can reason about directly.

The book prints 17.92 with the sum running to \(M\). It should run over all \(N\) points. Also, 17.89 has no linear term: the "\(\sum\alpha_n\)" from 17.85 has moved into the constraint 17.92.

§17.3.4

The kernel trick: swap every dot product for a kernel

In the dual, the data show up only as inner products \(\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j\): in the objective, in the prediction, and in the bias. Replace each one with a Mercer kernel \(K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\) and you are running a linear SVM in the kernel's feature space \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)\) without ever computing \(\boldsymbol\phi\).

The prediction becomes \(f(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal S}\alpha_n\tilde y_nK(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf x)+\hat w_0\) (Eq. 17.93). The boundary is a straight hyperplane in feature space and a curve in input space. The cost of one prediction is one kernel evaluation per support vector.

Explain itAt your level

If you can't split them with a straight line, lift them up first.

Put red marbles in a ring around a pile of blue marbles on a table. No straight ruler can separate them. Now lift every marble by how far it is from the center: the outside red marbles go up high, the blue ones in the middle stay low. A flat sheet of paper slid in between separates them perfectly.

The trick: the SVM never actually has to lift the marbles. It only needs to know "how similar are these two marbles?" That similarity score is the kernel from §17.1, and it secretly knows about the lifting.

A curved boundary in 2-D can be a flat one in a higher-dimensional space. Take a point on a number line, \(x\), and turn it into two numbers \((x,x^2)\). Points far from 0 rise up a parabola and points near 0 stay low, so a slanted line in the new plane can separate "near 0" from "far from 0." The lift panel below does this.

The SVM's formulas only ever multiply points together: \(\mathbf x_i\cdot\mathbf x_j\). In the lifted space that product is \(x_ix_j+x_i^2x_j^2\), which you can compute straight from \(x_i\) and \(x_j\). A kernel is any formula like that. The RBF kernel corresponds to infinitely many lifted coordinates, and it still costs one exponential to compute.

Kernelize each place a dot product appears. Objective: \(\sum_n\alpha_n-\tfrac12\sum_{ij}\alpha_i\alpha_j\tilde y_i\tilde y_jK(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\). Prediction (17.93): \(f(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal S}\alpha_n\tilde y_nK(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf x)+\hat w_0\). Bias (17.94): average \(\tilde y_i-\sum_j\alpha_j\tilde y_jK(\mathbf x_j,\mathbf x_i)\) over support vectors.

\(\hat{\mathbf w}=\sum\alpha_n\tilde y_n\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_n)\) still exists in feature space, but you never form it. The dual stays a convex QP as long as \(K\) is PSD, because the matrix \(Q_{ij}=\tilde y_i\tilde y_jK_{ij}\) is then PSD.

This is 17.63 again: \(\alpha_i\) there is \(\alpha_i\tilde y_i\) here, and it is zero for non-support vectors. Kernels also work on strings and graphs (§17.1.2.4), where there's no \(\mathbf x\) to take a dot product of.

  • Representer theorem. For any loss plus \(\lVert f\rVert^2_{\mathcal H_K}\), the minimizer has the form \(\sum_n\beta_nK(\cdot,\mathbf x_n)\). The SVM's hinge loss makes \(\boldsymbol\beta\) sparse; squared loss (kernel ridge, GP mean) doesn't.
  • Cost. Training needs the Gram matrix: \(O(N^2)\) memory, \(O(N^2)\)–\(O(N^3)\) time. LIBSVM caches kernel rows and shrinks the active set. Past \(\sim10^5\) points, use random Fourier features or Nyström plus a linear SVM.
  • RBF hyperparameters. sklearn writes \(e^{-\gamma\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert^2}\), \(\gamma=1/2\ell^2\). Large \(\gamma\) (small \(\ell\)): every point is its own island and nearly all become SVs. Small \(\gamma\): close to linear.
  • Not probabilistic. \(f(\mathbf x)\) is a score, not a log-odds (Fig. 17.15). Platt scaling fits \(\sigma(af+b)\) on held-out data. The RVM and GP classifiers model probabilities directly.
  • Indefinite kernels. With a non-PSD "kernel" (e.g. sigmoid for some parameters), \(Q\) is indefinite, the dual is non-convex, and SMO finds a local stationary point.

Math, slowlyEquations 17.93 – 17.94

Kernelize the prediction

\[ f(\mathbf x)=\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\mathbf x+\hat w_0=\sum_{n\in\mathcal S}\alpha_n\tilde y_n\mathbf x_n^\top\mathbf x+\hat w_0=\sum_{n\in\mathcal S}\alpha_n\tilde y_nK(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf x)+\hat w_0 \tag{17.93} \]
  1. Start in a feature space: replace \(\mathbf x\) everywhere by \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)\). The dual (17.85) becomes \(\sum\alpha_n-\tfrac12\sum_{ij}\alpha_i\alpha_j\tilde y_i\tilde y_j\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_j)\).
  2. By Mercer's theorem (§17.1.1), \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_j)=K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\). Every appearance of \(\boldsymbol\phi\) is inside such a product, so \(\boldsymbol\phi\) disappears. Training needs only the \(N\times N\) Gram matrix.
  3. \(\hat{\mathbf w}=\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_n)\) may be infinite-dimensional, but \(\hat{\mathbf w}^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)=\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_nK(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf x)\) is a finite sum. That's 17.93. Hover over the Play box to see it evaluated.

Kernelize the bias

\[ \hat w_0=\frac1{|\mathcal S|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}\Big(\tilde y_i-\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal S}\hat\alpha_j\tilde y_j\mathbf x_j\Big)^\top\mathbf x_i\Big)=\frac1{|\mathcal S|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}\Big(\tilde y_i-\sum_{j\in\mathcal S}\hat\alpha_j\tilde y_jK(\mathbf x_j,\mathbf x_i)\Big) \tag{17.94} \]

For a soft margin, average over the on-margin set \(\mathcal M\) instead of \(\mathcal S\), as in 17.88. That's what the Play box does.

The lift panel, written out

With \(\boldsymbol\phi(x)=(x,x^2)\):

\[ K(a,b)=\boldsymbol\phi(a)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(b)=ab+a^2b^2,\qquad f(x)=\sum_n\alpha_n\tilde y_n(x_nx+x_n^2x^2)+\hat w_0=\hat w_1x+\hat w_2x^2+\hat w_0. \]

A line in the \((x,x^2)\) plane is a quadratic in \(x\), and its two roots are the two thresholds. For example \(a=2,b=3\): \(\boldsymbol\phi(a)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(b)=(2,4)\cdot(3,9)=6+36=42=2\cdot3+4\cdot9\).

Why the RBF can carve out any shape

With \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=\exp(-\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert^2/2\ell^2)\), \(f\) is a sum of Gaussian bumps centered on the support vectors, positive bumps for red and negative for blue, plus \(\hat w_0\). Small \(\ell\) means narrow bumps and a boundary that hugs individual points. Far from all support vectors, \(f(\mathbf x)\to\hat w_0\).
